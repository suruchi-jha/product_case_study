## What Each Teardown Examines

The scope varies by product, but the case studies commonly connect several parts of product work:

- **Users and problems:** target segments, personas, jobs to be done, pain points, and customer journeys.
- **Product and market context:** core experience, industry dynamics, competitors, and product evolution.
- **Business and strategy:** business model, marketplace participants, growth loops, and strategic trade-offs.
- **Measurement:** funnels, North Star Metrics, AARRR, and product-specific success metrics.
- **Experience quality:** UX and feature audits, friction points, and accessibility or trust considerations where relevant.
- **Prioritisation and proposals:** opportunity sizing, prioritisation frameworks, feature concepts, PRDs, MVPs, and roadmaps.
- **Validation:** experiment plans, risks, and measurable outcomes for proposed changes.

These are independent analyses, not company-approved product plans. Frameworks are used where they help answer the product question; a framework listed here does not imply that every case study applies every method.

## How I Think About Products

My approach is to move from understanding to action: define who the product serves and what problem matters, inspect the journey and business context, identify the highest-leverage friction, and propose a change that can be tested. Good product thinking has to account for both the user experience and the system around it, including business constraints, partners, operations, and unintended effects.

The case studies draw on public information and product analysis. Where figures are estimates or a proposal depends on an assumption, the relevant study should be used for that context; recommendations are hypotheses to validate, not claims about a company's internal roadmap.
